## 1. What the proposed analysis actually is

A three-way triangulation, Canadian-internal:

| Layer | Source | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard (normative)** | Digital Health Canada **Health Informatics Professional (HIP) Core Competencies** — 7 domains (Information Management; Technology Ecosystem; Clinical and Health Services; The Canadian Healthcare System; Healthcare Transformation; Project Management; Analysis and Evaluation); plus the Career Matrix (9 domains × 5 proficiency levels) and the CPHIMS-CA exam outline | What the profession *says* competence is |
| **Demand (revealed)** | Job postings. OECD (May 2025) already analyses **55.5 million** online postings across Canada/UK/US for digital and AI skills in health occupations; or scrape Canadian postings directly | What employers *ask for* |
| **Supply (taught)** | Canadian HI programmes (MSHI, UVic MHI, Waterloo MHIA, U of T MScAC, Ontario Tech, etc.) | What is *taught* |

The finding would be the **misalignment across the three** — the same *shape* as the African work (policy says X, training teaches Y), relocated to the competency level.

**They explicitly name your study as the missing work, four times:**

## 3. Blocker 2 — prior art on the demand side is dense (but US-centric)

Job-postings-vs-competency gap analysis is **well-established method**, overwhelmingly applied to the US:

- A Data-Driven Assessment of US Health Informatics Programs and Job Market (PMC8967484)
- What Industry Wants: Empirical Analysis of Health Informatics Job Postings (PMC8013781; n=206)
- Mapping Health Informatics Education and Industry Job Competencies (AMIA 2025, Yu et al.)
- Topic-modelling gap analysis against the 7 AMIA/CAHIIM domains (2017)
- Gap between BSc HI graduates and employer needs, Saudi Arabia (*BMC Med Educ* 2023)
- Mining 831 HI job advertisements (Hawaii)

**The gap you'd fill is genuine: none of these is Canadian,** and none uses a *national professional body's* competency framework as the normative standard. Canada is unusually well-suited — DHC has a published framework, a credential (CPHIMS-CA), and its own workforce report. So the contribution is "first Canadian application, using the national professional standard as the normative anchor," not "new method."

## 4. The genuinely strong idea inside this — the professionalisation comparator

This is better than the policy comparator you rejected, and it is **fair**:

| | Africa | Canada |
|---|---|---|
| Competency framework | IMIA/HELINA — **voluntary alignment** | DHC HIP Core Competencies — **professionally adopted** |
| Accreditation | **0 of 36** programmes have HI-specific professional accreditation; no pan-African HI accreditor | **CPHIMS-CA** credential exists; DHC is the professional body |
| Status | HI is *academically* institutionalised, **not professionally** | HI is **professionally** institutionalised |

**The premise this sets:** *does professionalisation change whether competency frameworks match employer demand?* Canada has the professionalisation infrastructure Africa lacks. If Canada's professionally-endorsed framework **also** diverges from employer demand, the finding generalises: **competency frameworks are normative artifacts, not descriptions of the labour market** — in rich and poor systems alike. That is a premise the African work can build on, and it is exactly the kind of claim your African findings cannot establish alone.

This is also the safest framing for a Canadian audience — see §5.

## 5. Why DHC would welcome this (the opposite of the "turned away" problem)

DHC's own **Report on Canada's Digital Health Workforce (Sept 2026)** identifies as its own gaps: *unclear role definitions, insufficient digital education, and a lack of workforce data.* It then recommends *coordination of education, professionalization of roles, AI-focused workforce strategies, and improved workforce data collection.*

**You would be supplying the evidence DHC has itself named as missing.** That is a supportive, not adversarial, posture — and it inverts the dynamic that made the Africa comparator unusable. Frame it as *"answering the workforce-data gap DHC identified,"* not *"evaluating DHC."*

## 8. Honest caveats

- **The instrument is not validated** — you've conceded this, and B should say so plainly. It establishes a premise, not a validated method.
- **Posting corpora are biased** toward formalised, well-resourced employers; they under-represent public-sector and clinical roles. State it.
- **"Competency" ≠ "competence."** A framework describes intent; a posting describes a wish-list; neither measures delivery. This is the same intended-vs-delivered caveat that governs Papers 1–3.
- **DHC's framework may be mid-revision** — check publication dates on the HIP competencies (2019 and 2022 versions both appear in search results) and cite the current version.
